The information in these accounts is summarised into financial reports and statements, with the main ones being the cash flow statement, income statement and balance sheet. accounting: a managerial and administrative tool for recording financial transactions, so that a summary of what has happened to business money can be traced contingencies : unanticipated events that can lead to financial difficulty. For a business to be well managed, it needs to have saved money for such events. Key Aspects of Financial Management Sourcing : the ability to secure funds and ensuring the appropriate application of these funds. This comes from equity and debt sources. Cash flow : the ‘lifeblood’ of the business. Managers need to anticipate the cash expenditure (including unforeseen expenses) to ensure that adequate income is received in the form of cash. Objectives of Financial Management For a business to achieve its long term goals, it must have a number of short term, specific objectives. Two objectives of financial management are to maximise the business’s: Profitability Profitability is the ability of a business to maximise its profits. To ensure this occurs a business must carefully monitor its revenue and pricing policies, costs and expenses, inventory levels and levels of assets. Liquidity Liquidity is the extent to which a business can meet its financial commitments in the short-term (less than 12 months). A business must have sufficient cash flow to meet its financial obligations or be able to convert current assets into cash quickly Cash shortfalls and excess of idle cash must be avoided as both involve loss of profitability for a business.

Activity: Complete a simple cashflow statement READ Case Study: SMEs and cash flow One of the biggest causes of failure among small businesses is running out of cash. Xero Small Business Insights provides a snapshot of the small business sector’s health. Their research and data, which is updated monthly, has shown that in recent years, in any given month, about half of Australian small businesses are cash flow negative, with more money going out the door than coming in. In fact, in February 2020, the percentage of Australian small businesses that were cash flow positive businesses was only 49.8 per cent. Their research also demonstrated that the percentage of businesses operating in the red is tied to how quickly they get paid by their customers. Late payments have continued to have an impact on small businesses, who are currently waiting an average of 34.6 days to be paid on a 30-day invoice. Almost a quarter of small businesses that don’t insist on upfront payments have to wait more than a month to be paid, despite the fact they offer 30-day credit terms. This can be hugely disruptive to cash flow. Many small business owners have revealed that they spend on average 12 days a year chasing unpaid invoices. Research has also found that 27 per cent of small businesses that don’t insist on upfront payments have been forced to take out loans or use credit to pay for their own expenses such as wages, rent or other outstanding bills. Fed up with late payments, John Franji and Anthony Daoud who own Flowrite Plumbing decided to swap 30-day payment terms for 14-day payment terms. This has helped them to manage their cash flow. Since their own suppliers offer them 30 days credit, they are able to purchase the materials they need to complete a job on credit and, by giving their customers only 14 days credit, they usually don’t need to use any of their own money to make their repayments. Reducing the payment terms has resulted in an improvement in debtor collections; however, around 50 per cent of their invoices still exceed those terms at any one time and need chasing. Some of their customers pay their debts on average 45 days late. ‘Cash flow and collecting payments is vital for us. If debt starts to build up, it can start to snowball and cause problems. We communicate our payment terms up front and chase payments as soon as they are overdue. Throughout the process, we aim to be friendly and helpful as we want the customer to continue with us. We’ve found that in our industry, it’s pretty standard to be chasing late invoices. This is one of the biggest burdens on our business and takes up a lot of our time,’ they said. Chasing late payments is time-consuming and frustrating, and has become a major issue for many small businesses, which affects their cash flow. The Small Business and Family Enterprise Ombudsman, Kate Carnell, revealed that the number one problem small businesses tell her about is the negative impact late payments have on cash flow. As a result, she conducted an inquiry and in 2017 she released the findings of her ‘Inquiry into Payment Times and Practices in Australia’. The inquiry found that almost half of all the small businesses surveyed were owed more than $20 000 from late payments and 14 per cent had more than $100 000 owing. Approximately one in two small businesses reported more than 40 per cent of their invoices were paid late in the 2016–17 financial year, which had a big impact on their cash flow.
