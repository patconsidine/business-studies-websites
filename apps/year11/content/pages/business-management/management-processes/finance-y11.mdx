<ReadingSection title="Topic focus">

**Finance** — how a business funds its activities: where money comes from, why certain lenders are chosen, and the costs, risks and benefits of different borrowings.

**Accounting** — recording financial transactions so money in and out can be traced over time, summarised in financial reports.

</ReadingSection>

<WorkbookSection title="Finance definitions">

- **Finance** — organising the business's money and ensuring effective use  
- **Accounting** — recording transactions to summarise what happened to business money  
- **Contingencies** — unanticipated events that can cause financial difficulty; businesses should reserve funds  
- **Profitability** — ability to maximise profit  
- **Liquidity** — ability to meet short-term financial commitments (under 12 months)  

</WorkbookSection>

<ReadingSection title="Objectives of financial management">

Short-term objectives support long-term goals. Two key objectives:

| Objective | Meaning |
|-----------|---------|
| **Profitability** | Maximise profit — monitor revenue, pricing, costs, inventory and assets |
| **Liquidity** | Enough cash (or convertible assets) to pay obligations; avoid cash shortfalls and excess idle cash |

**Key aspects:** **Sourcing** funds (equity and debt); managing **cash flow** as the lifeblood of the business.

</ReadingSection>

<ReadingSection title="Cash flow statements">

A **cash flow statement** shows cash coming in and going out. Sufficient cash is crucial for wages, suppliers and government payments.

**Why they matter:**

- Show **liquidity** — can the business pay debts quickly?  
- Support **budgeting** — when to pay bills  
- Reveal patterns that threaten survival  

**SME cash flow — key facts:**

- Running out of cash is a leading cause of small business failure  
- In recent years, roughly **half** of Australian small businesses were cash-flow negative in any given month  
- **Late payments** disrupt cash flow — average wait can exceed 30-day terms  
- Case study: **Flowrite Plumbing** switched from 30-day to **14-day** payment terms to improve collections  

</ReadingSection>

<ReadingSection title="Income statement">

Also called **profit and loss statement** or **statement of financial performance** — measures financial performance over a period (e.g. one year).

Shows: revenue earned, expenses incurred, and whether a **profit or loss** resulted.

| Step | Calculation |
|------|-------------|
| **Revenue** | Income from sales (+ other income such as dividends) |
| **Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)** | Cost to make goods sold |
| **Gross profit** | Revenue − COGS |
| **Expenses** | Rent, wages, etc. |
| **Net profit** | Gross profit − expenses |

Managers use low net profit to ask: are **expenses too high** or **revenue too low**?

</ReadingSection>

<WorkbookSection title="Income statement formulas">

- **Gross profit** = Revenue − COGS  
- **Net profit** = Gross profit − Expenses

<ReadingSection title="Balance sheet">

Shows the business's **financial position on a given day** (not over a period). Both sides must **balance**.

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Assets** | What the business owns — **current** (< 12 months) and **non-current** |
| **Liabilities** | What the business owes — current and non-current |
| **Owner's equity** | Money invested in the business; balances the equation |

**Why it matters:**

- **Liquidity** — do current assets cover current liabilities?  
- **Debt to equity** — how much debt vs owner's equity?  
- Supports better financial decisions and planning  

</ReadingSection>

<ReadingSection title="Break-even analysis (revisited)">

Break-even = sales level where **total revenue equals total costs** (fixed + variable).

**Break-even quantity** = FC ÷ (P − VC per unit)

**Example — Smell The Roses Candles:** price $5, variable cost $2.50, fixed costs $500.

Break-even quantity = 500 ÷ (5 − 2.50) = **200 units**.

</ReadingSection>
